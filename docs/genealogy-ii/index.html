The record

  • 275 individuals across 6 generation columns; 61 marriages; 214 parent–child links.
  • Transcribed from the plate and verified against it. Clan descent is matrilineal, which gives every bracket an independent check; all 61 marriages pass it.

Navigating this chart

  • A + at the start of a line marks a spouse, printed on the line below the person they married.
  • F. and M. give the sex of each person as printed.
  • The horizontal leader rule runs from a person to the bracket holding their children; the bracket itself hangs on the mother’s line.
  • A number after a name is the person’s age when the data was collected. d. means they had already died when Parsons recorded the genealogy, during her fieldwork of 1918–19; where she knew the year she gives it, as in d. 1913.
  • Every person number is a stable link — #p1 addresses person 1’s first printed line, and the address bar keeps it while you read.
  • The chart region can be focused and panned with the arrow keys; on a narrow screen, scroll sideways to follow the later generations.
  • With JavaScript: with the chart focused, press / to find a person by number or name; selecting a person number opens its register card, and Esc closes it. The Theme and Scale controls remember your choice.

Editorial notes

  • Clan membership is matrilineal: every sibling group carries its mother's clan. That rule was used to verify the bracket readings against the plate.
  • A dash in place of a name means the plate printed no name.
  • The dashed rules separating the three descent blocks are an editorial addition, not on the plate — one above each block after the first. They mark the three founding couples, 1+2, 154+155 and 232+233.
  • The plate numbers two different people 101 — a woman, Naaʼdᶦityʼi of the Water clan, and a man whose name it prints as a dash. Both rows print 101 here, because the chart prints what the plate prints. They are separate entries in the register and separate person cards; the numbering, not the edition, is what is ambiguous. One consequence a reader will meet: typing “101” into Find goes to the first of the two, since the number alone cannot say which is meant. Searching the name, or opening either row from the register, reaches each of them exactly.
  • 31 and 32 sit inside the bracket holding 9 and 10’s children, at the children’s indent, with no rule joining them to it — and that gap is the plate’s, reproduced here rather than tidied away. Every other person in a bracket is reached by a short horizontal stub off its vertical rule; 31’s row is the one the rule passes without stopping. So Parsons prints him among 9’s children while declining to say he is one, and this edition prints him where she does and says no more. His clan is Water, as 9 and 10 are, so the matrilineal check that tests every other bracket on this plate can neither confirm nor deny it; the absent stub is the whole of the evidence. His wife 32 and their child 97 follow from his line as usual.
  • Six people are drawn twice on the plate and are drawn once here, with the plate’s own “For descendants, see above” standing in for the repeat: 13, 14, 53, 54, 125 and 126. Person 169 repeats within the lower block in the same way.
  • Where a person is drawn twice, the plate sometimes sets the name differently in the two places — 13 (Dzia˙ʼyotsʼa / Tsiaiutsa), 54 (Ma˙ʼrani / Ma˙ʼran˙i) and 125 (Gowaʼk˙ʼd˙yăiʼ / Gowaʼkʼad˙zăiʼ). Both settings were read at high magnification and both are what the plate prints, so this edition carries both rather than choosing between them. A parenthesis after a name on this table therefore means one of three things: the second setting of a repeat person’s name, as here; an English name the plate itself prints in parentheses, at 27, 42, 43 and 140; or the second half of a pair the plate joins with a brace, at 14.
  • Parsons’s cross-references from this plate into Genealogy I do not all use the numbering Genealogy I was finally printed with. Checked by name, sex and clan, they are exact through Genealogy I’s person 53 — twenty-two matches — and one too high from its person 66 onward, on seven more. Nothing between its 54 and 65 is referenced from here, so where the displacement begins cannot be settled from these two plates. The references are reproduced exactly as printed and are deliberately not linked: a link would have to resolve the displacement, and resolving it silently would send a reader to the wrong person. This is the same phenomenon as Genealogy I’s own misprint at person 67, which Table 2 independently numbers 68 at 60.
  • One cross-reference carries Parsons’s own question mark — 199 prints “See Gen. I, 43 (?)”. It is the only reference on the plate with one, and the only one in the exact range that does not match by name. The “(?)” is hers and is reproduced.
  • Three lines print a word in the clan position that is not a clan — White at 179, Mexican at 183, Mohave at 243. Each is recorded where the plate sets it, not reinterpreted. Person 161 is marked “M.-F.”, a notation used nowhere else on the plate; the sex is left as printed rather than resolved.
  • Persons 160 and 163 carry cross-references into Genealogy III, which is not yet transcribed. Those references are printed as the plate has them and are not links, because a link must not promise a page that does not exist.
  • The plate draws a short rule from 235+236’s sibling bracket to the ‘+’ line of 255, which it does for no other spouse line. It cannot mean 255 is their child: every child of 236 is Water, her own clan, and 255 is Eagle. It is recorded as an observation of the plate, not as descent.
  • The generation ruler above the chart, the linked person numbers, and the register below the chart are editorial apparatus of this edition, not part of the plate.
